Sourdough fermentation

The complete guide: microbiology, enzymes, chemistry and the physics of dough rise

1. Who lives in a sourdough starter

A starter is a symbiosis of two groups of microorganisms that work as a pair:

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB)

The main residents of a starter. There are 10-100 times more of them than yeast.

Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis L. plantarum L. brevis Leuconostoc

What they do: produce lactic acid and acetic acid, which give flavor and aroma and protect against mold.

Yeast

Responsible for the rise — they produce CO₂ (the gas that inflates the dough).

Kazachstania humilis Saccharomyces cerevisiae Candida milleri

What they do: ferment sugars into CO₂ + ethanol. The gas lifts the dough; the alcohol evaporates during baking.

Why do they get along?

LAB and yeast don't compete for food — they eat different things. L. sanfranciscensis feeds on maltose (a disaccharide from flour), but when it breaks it down it releases glucose, which it doesn't use itself — the yeast picks it up. This is called cross-feeding — mutual feeding [4][6].

On top of that, the acidic environment (pH 3.5–4.5) created by the bacteria suppresses pathogenic microbes and commercial yeast, while the "native" yeast is adapted to it [5].

2. Enzymes — the invisible workers

Enzymes are catalyst proteins. They aren't alive, but nothing works without them. Enzymes are present in the flour itself and are also produced by the microorganisms:

Enzyme Source What it does Result
Amylase Flour Breaks down starch → maltose + glucose Food for bacteria and yeast
Protease Flour + bacteria Breaks down gluten proteins → amino acids Soft crumb, aroma during baking (Maillard reaction)
Phytase Bacteria + flour Breaks down phytic acid Minerals (iron, zinc, magnesium) become absorbable
Xylanase Flour (especially rye) Breaks down pentosans (arabinoxylans) Affects crumb structure and water retention
Lipase Flour Breaks down fats → fatty acids Aroma, but in excess — rancidity

Important: enzyme activity depends on temperature and acidity. Amylase works best at 60-70°C (during baking!), and protease at 35-45°C. In a starter at 22-28°C the enzymes work slowly but steadily — this gives long fermentation and deep flavor [6].

3. The chemistry of fermentation

The main reactions

Alcoholic fermentation (yeast):

C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose) → 2 C₂H₅OH (ethanol) + 2 CO₂ ↑

It is the CO₂ that lifts the dough. The gluten network (in wheat flour) or pentosans (in rye) hold the gas inside.

Homofermentative lactic acid fermentation (L. plantarum and others):

C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 CH₃CHOHCOOH (lactic acid)

A mild, creamy, slightly tangy flavor. Dominates at high temperature (30°C+).

Heterofermentative lactic acid fermentation (L. sanfranciscensis, L. brevis):

C₆H₁₂O₆ → CH₃CHOHCOOH + C₂H₅OH + CO₂ ↑

Produces lactic acid, ethanol and CO₂ alike. Gives a more complex, vinegary flavor. Dominates at low temperature (18-22°C) [4][5].

The acid ratio = the bread's flavor

QF (Quotient of Fermentation) — the ratio of lactic acid to acetic acid:

QFFlavorHow to get it
QF > 5Mild, milkyWarm fermentation (28-32°C), liquid starter
QF 2-5BalancedMedium temperature (22-26°C)
QF < 2Sharp, vinegaryCold fermentation (4-18°C), stiff starter

[4][5]

4. The phases of a starter's rise

Phase 1: Lag phase (0 — 1.5 h)

The microorganisms "wake up" and adapt to the fresh portion of flour. The flour's enzymes (amylase) begin breaking starch down into simple sugars. There is almost no visible rise.

Amylase starch → maltose → L. sanfranciscensis starts feeding

Phase 2: Exponential growth (1.5 h — 70% of peak)

Yeast and bacteria multiply rapidly. CO₂ production ramps up. The dough starts to grow noticeably. The first bubbles appear. Acidity drops the pH from ~6 to ~4.5.

Yeast sugars → CO₂ + ethanol   LAB maltose → lactic acid + glucose

Phase 3: Peak (70% — 100%)

Maximum volume. The starter is full of bubbles, the dome is rounded. The sugar reserve runs out, acidity reaches pH 3.5-4.0. Protease begins to break down gluten — the structure weakens.

Ready to use: it's best to use it at 80% of the rise (before the peak), when the microbes are still active but the gluten isn't broken down yet [3].

Phase 4: Decline (after the peak)

The sugars are gone. The yeast slows down. Acidity keeps rising. Protease breaks down the gluten network — the gas escapes and the starter falls. Liquid (hooch) appears on the surface — alcohol + water.

The starter is still alive but weakened — bread made from it will turn out flat and too sour.

5. Wheat vs rye: what's the difference

The type of flour dramatically affects how a starter behaves — and it's not just about speed:

Parameter Wheat Rye
Dough structure Gluten network holds the gas Pentosans (arabinoxylans) hold gas and water
Enzyme activity Moderate High — more amylase, faster starch breakdown
Fermentation speed Baseline 20-25% faster [1][3]
Gas production (CO₂) High (100%) Low (~55% of wheat) [1][2]
Visual rise x2.5–3.0 x1.7–2.2 [1][3]
Peak duration 60-90 minutes 30-60 minutes (falls faster)
Main bacteria L. sanfranciscensis, L. plantarum L. sanfranciscensis, L. pontis, L. reuteri [5]
Flavor profile Mild, milky More pronounced, with vinegary notes

Why is rye faster but lower?

Faster — because rye flour contains more free sugars and active enzymes. The microbes don't need to "warm up" for long — the food is available right away [6].

Lower — because rye flour has no full-fledged gluten. Instead of an elastic gluten network, the gas is held by pentosans — water-soluble polysaccharides. They form a viscous gel structure that holds CO₂ less well, so the gas escapes sooner. The visual rise of a rye starter is partly deceptive — it "swells" thanks to the water retention of the pentosans, not because of gas [1][2].

6. The effect of temperature

Temperature is the most powerful lever for controlling fermentation. The rule:

Every +5.5°C halves the time to peak

This relates to the Arrhenius rule — the rate of biochemical reactions doubles for roughly every 10°C increase, and for a starter the coefficient is even steeper [4].

Temperature Wheat 1:2:2 What happens
16°C~14 hSlow, more acetic acid, complex flavor
22°C~7 hBalanced flavor, the standard
28°C~3.5 hFast, more lactic acid, mild flavor
35°C~1.5 hVery fast, risk of over-acidity, bacteria dominate
4°C (fridge)PausedFermentation almost stops, but enzymes keep working

Temperature and flavor

Warm (28-32°C): homofermentative LAB dominate → more lactic acid → mild, yogurt-like flavor.

Cold (18-22°C): heterofermentative LAB dominate → more acetic acid → sharp, complex, the "real" sourdough flavor [4][5].

Fridge (4°C): the microbes sleep, but the enzymes (protease, amylase) keep working. That's why a cold proof improves the flavor and texture of the bread without the risk of over-acidity.

7. The effect of the feeding ratio

The starter:flour:water ratio determines how much "food" the microbes get and how fast they eat it:

  • 1:1:1 — many microbes, little food → fast peak (3-5 h), fast decline. Good for baking in 4-6 hours.
  • 1:2:2 — the standard. A balance of speed and strength. Peak in 5-7 h.
  • 1:5:5 — few microbes, lots of food → slow growth, high peak. For an overnight cycle (9-12 h).
  • 1:10:10 — "for the weekend". Very slow (14-17 h). The starter holds its peak longer.

How the ratio affects flavor

More flour = longer fermentation = more time for the enzymes to work = a more complex flavor. A 1:5:5 starter at 20°C will give more aromatic bread than 1:1:1 at 30°C, even though both will be at their peak [3][4].

8. The effect of hydration

Starter hydration — the ratio of water to flour as a percentage:

HydrationConsistencyEffect
50-60%Stiff (like dough)More acetic acid, slower, sharper flavor
100%Standard (like thick sour cream)Balance of lactic and acetic
125-150%LiquidMore lactic acid, milder, faster

The reason: in a stiff medium it's harder for bacteria to move, CO₂ diffuses worse, and the heterofermentative LAB (which produce acetic acid) gain the advantage [5].

9. What else happens during fermentation

🛡️ Protection against mold

LAB produce bacteriocins and organic acids that suppress the growth of mold and pathogenic bacteria. Sourdough bread keeps longer than yeast bread [5].

🧬 Gluten breakdown

Bacterial protease partly breaks down gluten during long fermentation. Sourdough doesn't make bread gluten-free, but it can lower its reactivity for people with mild sensitivity [4].

⚡ Mineral bioavailability

Phytase breaks down phytic acid — the "trap" for minerals. In ordinary bread iron, zinc and calcium are bound and poorly absorbed. In sourdough they are free [4][6].

🍞 Maillard reaction

Amino acids (from protease) + sugars (from amylase) produce the Maillard reaction during baking — a brown crust and hundreds of aromatic compounds. The longer the fermentation, the more "raw material" there is for Maillard [6].
